60,000 museum instruments, sorted by a computer that has never been told what an instrument is

What the project does, what came out, and why the numbers mean what they mean. No prior knowledge assumed.

Step 1The raw material

Museums that belong to MIMO (Musical Instrument Museums Online) have photographed their instruments and put the photos online with a catalogue card. The card says what the object is called (clarinet, oud, slit drum), where it comes from, which museum holds it, and a code from a classification system that organologists have used since 1914, called Hornbostel-Sachs. That code sorts instruments by how the sound is made: a vibrating string, a vibrating skin, air, or the body of the object itself. We harvested all of it, then removed placeholder images, duplicate photos and catalogue records of parts such as bows and mouthpieces.

Step 2Turning a photo into a point on a map

A computer cannot compare 2 photos directly. So each photo is first handed to a vision model (we use one called DINOv2) that has looked at hundreds of millions of ordinary pictures and learned, on its own, what shapes, textures and parts look like. For each photo it writes down 768 numbers that describe what it sees. Photos that look alike get similar numbers.

You can think of those numbers as coordinates. Every photo becomes a dot on a map, and dots that are close together are photos that look alike. The model was never told what a violin or a drum is. It only knows "these 2 look similar".

Step 3Clustering: drawing circles around the crowds

Clustering means asking the computer to find the crowds on that map by itself. We tell it only how many groups to make, say 20. It then moves 20 markers around until every dot is assigned to its nearest marker and the markers sit in the middle of their crowds. That procedure is called k-means, and k is the number of groups. Nobody tells it what the groups should be. It has no labels, no catalogue cards, nothing. It just finds where the dots bunch up.

The result is 20 groups of photos. The question of the whole project is: do these blind groups mean anything? Do they line up with how experts sort instruments, with how instruments are named, with where they come from, or with how they sound?

Step 4Scoring the groups against the experts

To check whether the blind groups mean anything, we compare them with a label we hid from the computer, for example the top level of the Hornbostel-Sachs code (string, skin, air, body, electric). The score we use is called AMI, adjusted mutual information. It runs from 0 to 1.

AMI 1 means the groups and the labels are the same sorting: every group holds exactly one label and every label sits in exactly one group.
AMI 0 means the groups tell you nothing about the labels. Sorting the photos at random gives 0, because the score has the luck of random matches taken out ("adjusted").
In between means partial overlap. Groups that each hold mostly one label, with some spill, land around 0.5.

A useful way to picture it: a librarian who cannot read is asked to shelve books by look alone. AMI measures how closely the shelves end up matching the catalogue, after subtracting whatever match pure luck would give.

We also run a "cheat test" for comparison. We let a simple model peek at the labels of 80 percent of the photos, learn from them, and then guess the remaining 20 percent. This is called a linear probe. It tells us how much of the label is readable in the 768 numbers at all. Blind clustering can never beat it, so it is the ceiling, and the distance from the blind score to the ceiling is the room for better methods.

Finding 3It is the object, not the camera

A fair objection: maybe the computer groups by photo style, since each museum uses one photographer and one backdrop. We checked by clustering inside each museum separately, so the photo style is held fixed.

Finding 4Instruments that look alike sound alike

For 963 objects the museum also recorded the instrument being played. We ran the same trick on the sound: a model that turns audio into numbers (CLAP), then blind grouping. Then we asked: when 2 objects landed in the same photo group, do their recordings sound alike? You can listen for yourself on the seen-and-heard page.

So whatWhat the project would be

The blind groups are a typology of instruments made from how they look, with no expert involved. The project builds that typology properly (at several levels of detail, from a few groups to hundreds), lets language name each group (the majority catalogue name, or a model that writes captions), and then compares the result with the expert trees. Where they agree, the expert split is visible in form. Where they disagree, we can say exactly which instruments the camera sees differently from the organologist, and whether the ear sides with the camera or the expert.

Two things make it publishable rather than a class exercise. Nobody has scored image groups of instruments against the Hornbostel-Sachs tree before, and the data are usable under a non-commercial licence (60,000 photos, 1,000 recordings, 2 expert taxonomies, 129 names), so anyone can check the claim.

GlossaryThe words, in one line each

embedding
the 768 numbers a vision model writes down for a photo; the photo's coordinates on the map
DINOv2, CLAP
the models that turn photos (DINOv2) and audio (CLAP) into numbers; both were trained without any instrument labels
clustering, k-means
finding the crowds on the map with no labels; k is how many groups you ask for
AMI
0 to 1 score of how well blind groups match a hidden label, with luck removed
linear probe
the cheat test that peeks at labels; the ceiling blind clustering cannot pass
AUC
0.5 to 1 score of how often same-group pairs beat different-group pairs; 0.5 is a coin flip
Hornbostel-Sachs
the 1914 expert classification of instruments by how the sound is made, written as a code like 321.322
MIMO
Musical Instrument Museums Online, the network whose photos, cards and recordings we use
